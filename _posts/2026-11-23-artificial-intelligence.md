The science-fiction author Arthur C Clarke once wrote that 'any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic'. Perhaps this has never been truer than in the current age of artificial intelligence [AI]. The term alone conjures up wondrous ideas of machines that are not only extremely capable but conscious, just like us. AI companies are more than happy to encourage these ideas in order to boost hype for their products. But how ought we, as Christians, think about these claims, or understand our own experiences with this technology that can seem both all-knowing and very human?

Perhaps the best way to sort this out is to demystify what 'artificial intelligence' actually is and how, it really works. If we can move past the 'magic', hopefully we can see AI for what it really is, and make better decisions about its appropriate uses, limitations, possibilities, and dangers.

Let me start by addressing the term 'artificial intelligence'. The term I prefer is 'machine learning', which describes more clearly what AI actually is. That is, machines that are built to learn (by some definition of that term) to do a particular task. 'Intelligence' is a loaded term that quickly leads us down thorny paths, asking unhelpful questions such as, 'Is it conscious?' and 'Does it have feelings?' 'Learning', on the other hand, has us asking better questions: What is it learning to do? How does it learn? Within what parameters does it learn?

Before we ask how a machine learns, let's first ask what it is learning to do. To take a simple example, let's consider 'Large Language Models' [LLMs] --- the engine driving common tools like ChatGPT. These systems are trained to take some text and predict the most likely next word in a sentence. For example, after 'The capital of France is...', it will probably predict the word 'Paris' (but it may also predict another word, such as 'beautiful'). This process is repeated over and over again, predicting one word at a time, until the program decides it's finished.

How does it make this guess? Imagine words are carefully spaced out on a table. In this space not only are similar words arranged together, but directions between words can also convey meaning, so that, for example, the movement from 'France' to 'Paris' is similar to the movement from 'Germany' to 'Berlin'. It's hard to imagine how this arrangement would look on a two-dimensional table, but in practice these machines use many more dimensions.[^dimensions] Once this arrangement of words exists (called 'embedding'), it's the job of the machine to move through this space, based on all the input words, until it lands on the next word to produce. That's called the 'transform' phase --- the 'T' in 'ChatGPT'.

The machine is trained (or 'learns') by first calculating the word-embedding, and then the parameters that guide the transformations.[^parameters] This is driven by mathematics, where the parameters are slowly adjusted over many iterations of passing training data through the machine, and comparing its output to what the human trainers are looking for.

This is a simplification of a very complicated system, and I hope I haven't lost you. But how is understanding any of this at all useful for Christians and the way in which we think about 'artificial intelligence'?

Firstly, we can understand that these are just machines using mathematics and computation to produce output and no matter how life-like the images or sounds they produce, they're no more conscious or alive than a television. We tend to humanise physical objects (I have a much-loved teddy bear, for example) but no matter how human AI may appear, we should not become confused by this. Our tendency to impute human characteristics to AI and to relate to it as such, probably says more about our collective understanding of what humans are than of the true nature of these machines we have created. Human beings are not merely meat-machines, and while consciousness and intelligence may distinguish us from the animal kingdom by degrees, it is our having a soul, and bearing the image of God, that truly separates us from it in nature and dignity ([Genesis 2:7](http://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+2:7)).

Secondly, notice how critical the training data is to the production of these machines. This is the only subjective part of the system, along with other guidelines fed into the machines that guide their output. None of this is made public, and there have been many examples that demonstrate that these inputs are not viewpoint-neutral. It would be a mistake to assume that the machines are purely rational and objective, even if the makers seem to be trustworthy. We should never accept claims on face value alone, but should verify everything with trusted external sources. This is never more important than when dealing with the things of God and his revelation, the Bible
([1 Thessalonians 5:21](http://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Thessalonians+5:21), [2 Peter 3:16](http://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2+Peter+3:16)).

Finally, although these may just be machines and tools, they do increasingly engage in very human endeavours: writing (thinking?), art (feeling?), and even offering companionship (relationships?) and advice (wisdom?). It's worth asking ourselves what human activities we can afford to outsource to machines without losing, in some sense, what actually makes us human. To do any less is to sell our humanity short. God made us for a purpose, in His image --- not our machines. This purpose is not to merely exist and enjoy, but also to labour and serve in love, both Him and one another. This is no better exemplified than in Jesus' own incarnation ([John 1:14](http://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+1:14), [Philippians 2:7](http://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Philippians+2:7)), demonstrating the value and dignity in a human life of love and service. Let's not lose sight of those things that can only truly be done by us as the image-bearers of God. And let's be faithful in our service of that duty.
